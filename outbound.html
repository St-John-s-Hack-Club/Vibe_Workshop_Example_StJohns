A few legendary journeys

Our cosmic messengers

Some spacecraft visited planets. Others studied comets and asteroids. A handful are now travelling through interstellar space.

Launched 1977 · Interstellar mission

Voyager 1

After visiting Jupiter and Saturn, Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause in 2012. It is the most distant human-made spacecraft and still sends limited scientific data.

Launched 1977 · Interstellar mission

Voyager 2

Voyager 2 is the only spacecraft to have visited Uranus and Neptune. It crossed the heliopause in 2018 and continues its journey outward.

Launched 2006 · Kuiper Belt

New Horizons

New Horizons made the first close flyby of Pluto in 2015, revealing a surprisingly diverse icy world. It later flew past the Kuiper Belt object Arrokoth in 2019.

Launched 1972 · Historic pioneer

Pioneer 10

Pioneer 10 was the first spacecraft to travel through the asteroid belt and make direct observations of Jupiter. Its last signal reached Earth in 2003.

Launched 1997 · Saturn system

Cassini–Huygens

Cassini orbited Saturn for 13 years. Its Huygens probe landed on Titan in 2005—the first landing in the outer Solar System.

Perseverance

A message aboard

The Golden Record

A tiny introduction to Earth.

Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 each carry a gold-plated copper record containing sounds, music, greetings in 55 languages and images chosen to represent life and culture on Earth. It was designed as a message for any possible discoverer—not as a practical way to contact extraterrestrial life.

Important distinction

What does “leaving the Solar System” mean?

Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 have entered interstellar space by crossing the heliopause—the boundary where the solar wind's influence gives way to the interstellar environment. That does not mean they have left the Solar System in every scientific definition: the distant Oort Cloud, if it exists as predicted, extends much farther out. Space is full of boundaries, and the answer depends on which one we mean.
